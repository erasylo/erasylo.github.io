PLS 360 — Research Proposal, Part One

Does visibility create obligation?

TikTok posting frequency and the felt duty to share discrimination against women — Nazarbayev University

Research Question

The question driving this study

Does regular TikTok posting among female Nazarbayev University students increase their sense of obligation to share instances of discrimination against women on social media?

Scoped to female NU students who currently use TikTok, asking about their present, day-to-day experience of the platform — not a historical period or a single triggering event.

Epistemological Positioning

Why this is a positivist study

"Sense of obligation" is treated as an observable, measurable attitude that varies systematically with an identifiable independent variable — posting frequency — rather than a meaning that must be interpreted contextually. The hypothesis specifies a directional relationship and a discrete causal mechanism, calling for a stance that treats social reality as governed by regularities that can be tested, generalized, and potentially falsified.

Practically: students can be grouped by posting frequency, and their self-reported obligation measured and compared across groups using consistent survey items — an approach that assumes attitudes can be operationalized and compared.

Contribution

What this study adds

Existing research treats platform "engagement" as a broad, undifferentiated category. This study isolates one specific behavior — posting frequency — as a predictor of one specific attitude — felt obligation to share discrimination content — allowing a more precise test of the visibility-to-responsibility mechanism than prior work has offered.

It also extends a literature built almost entirely on Western, Southeast Asian, South Asian, and Chinese contexts to a Central Asian university population, a setting with a distinct social and political environment around gender discourse.

Literature Review

Five studies this proposal builds on

  1. Literat & Kligler-Vilenchik (2023) establish TikTok as a legitimate space for youth political voice — but not why individual users feel compelled to use it.
  2. NCA study on TikTok and Black political consumerism links frequent platform use to activism orientation and identity — the same logic this study applies to gender.
  3. "It's Viral!" (ACM) notes prior work hadn't explained why some users produce activist content over others — the exact gap this study's IV/DV design targets.
  4. #YesAllWomen research shows sharing discrimination content is socially costly, not neutral — establishing "obligation to share" as a meaningful outcome.
  5. Vachhani (2024), networked feminism describes visibility becoming tied to a felt duty toward other women — the phenomenon this study operationalizes.
Theory and Hypotheses

The mechanism and the hypothesis

Independent Variable

Frequency of TikTok posting — regular vs. infrequent posters

Dependent Variable

Self-reported sense of obligation to share gendered-discrimination content

H1: Female NU students who post regularly on TikTok will report a stronger sense of obligation to share instances of discrimination against women on social media than those who post infrequently.

Mechanism: Frequent posting builds a visible online audience and identity, fostering a self-perception of "having a platform" — creating an internalized responsibility to use it for causes one cares about.
