PLS 360 — Research Proposal, Part One

Does visibility create obligation?

TikTok posting frequency and the felt duty to share discrimination against women — Nazarbayev University

Question Positioning Contribution Literature Theory & Hypotheses AI Transparency Reflection

Research Question

The question driving this study

Does regular TikTok posting among female Nazarbayev University students increase their sense of obligation to share instances of discrimination against women on social media?

The question is scoped to female NU students who currently use TikTok, and asks about their present, day-to-day experience of the platform — not a historical period or a single triggering event.

Epistemological Positioning

Why this is a positivist study

This study treats "sense of obligation" as an observable, measurable attitude that varies systematically with an identifiable independent variable — posting frequency — rather than as a meaning that must be interpreted contextually. The hypothesis below specifies a directional relationship and a discrete causal mechanism, which calls for an epistemological stance that treats social reality as governed by regularities that can be tested against evidence, generalized across a defined population, and potentially falsified.

Practically, this fits the design: female NU students can be grouped by posting frequency, and their self-reported obligation can be measured and compared across groups using consistent survey items — an approach that assumes attitudes can be operationalized and compared, the core commitment of positivism.

Contribution

What this study adds

Existing research treats platform "engagement" as a broad, undifferentiated category. This study isolates one specific behavior — posting frequency — as a predictor of one specific attitude — felt obligation to share discrimination content — allowing a more precise test of the visibility-to-responsibility mechanism than prior work has offered.

It also extends a literature built almost entirely on Western, Southeast Asian, South Asian, and Chinese contexts to a Central Asian university population, a setting with a distinct social and political environment around gender discourse that has received little attention. Together, these moves test whether a mechanism observed elsewhere holds in a setting where public discussion of gender discrimination carries different social risks.

Literature Review

Five studies this proposal builds on

  1. Literat & Kligler-Vilenchik (2023), on youth political expression on TikTok, establish the platform as a legitimate space for political voice among young people — but stop short of explaining why individual users feel compelled to use that voice.
  2. National Communication Association study on TikTok and Black political consumerism links frequent platform use to activism orientation and racial identity, suggesting engagement is bound up with self-perception — the same identity logic this study applies to gender.
  3. "It's Viral!" (ACM, TikTok activism motivations) notes prior work documents that TikTok hosts activist content but not why some users produce it over others — the exact gap this study's IV/DV design targets.
  4. #YesAllWomen research (Humanities & Social Sciences Communications) shows that sharing discrimination content on social media is socially costly — met with both support and backlash — establishing "obligation to share" as a meaningful outcome worth explaining, not a neutral one.
  5. Vachhani (2024), networked feminism describes how visibility within digital feminist activism becomes tied to a felt duty toward other women — the broader phenomenon this study operationalizes into a testable mechanism.

Theory and Hypotheses

The mechanism and the hypothesis

Independent Variable

Frequency of TikTok posting — regular posters vs. infrequent posters

Dependent Variable

Self-reported sense of obligation to share gendered-discrimination content on social media

H1: Female NU students who post regularly on TikTok will report a stronger sense of obligation to share instances of discrimination against women on social media than those who post infrequently.

Causal mechanism: Frequent posting builds a visible online audience and identity. That visibility fosters a self-perception of "having a platform," which creates an internalized responsibility to use that platform for causes the poster cares about — here, gendered discrimination — producing a stronger felt obligation to share such content.
